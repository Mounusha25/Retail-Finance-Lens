# Retail Finance Lens — Performance & Working Capital Analytics

## Analyst report — Walmart, Target, Costco, and Kroger

**Prepared:** 2026-10-15  
**Decision question:** Which retailer is growing profitably, managing inventory efficiently, and converting profit into cash?  
**Scope:** Latest five completed annual periods and twelve standalone quarters per company, based on latest-known SEC filings.

---

## Executive conclusion

**Among Walmart, Target, and Costco, Costco presents the strongest combination of growth and operating efficiency.** Its latest net sales grew 10.1%, inventory turned 14.1×, and the cash conversion cycle was only 1.0 day. Walmart remains the peer group's largest absolute cash generator, producing $14.9B of free cash flow. Target retained the highest operating margin at 4.9%, but its -2.0% sales decline and slower inventory turns make the quality of that margin the most important issue to investigate.

This is not a single-winner story. Costco leads on momentum and working-capital speed; Walmart leads on cash scale; Target shows that higher margin does not automatically imply stronger growth or inventory productivity.

## Findings and financial implications

### 1. Costco combines the fastest growth with the strongest inventory productivity

Costco's latest net sales increased 10.1% to $297.2B. Gross margin was 11.1% and operating margin was 3.9%, while inventory turned 14.1×—well ahead of Walmart and Target (Kroger's grocery model is compared separately below). Its 1.0-day cash conversion cycle indicates that very little cash remained tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. [SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/909832/000090983226000093/cost-20260830.htm)

**Implication.** The low-margin model is economically attractive because sales velocity and supplier financing reduce the working capital required to support growth. Operating cash flow of $15.8B covered capex of $6.4B and left $9.4B of free cash flow. The main analytical question is therefore not whether Costco can move inventory, but whether membership economics and warehouse expansion can sustain this pace without weakening returns on incremental capital.

### 2. Walmart is the cash-flow leader, but its liquidity profile deserves context

Walmart generated $41.6B of operating cash flow and $14.9B of free cash flow—the highest absolute amount in the group. OCF/net income of 1.9× supports the quality of reported earnings. Net sales grew 4.7%, while inventory turnover reached 9.3× and the cash conversion cycle was 3.3 days. [SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/104169/000010416926000055/wmt-20260131.htm)

**Implication.** Walmart's scale produces exceptional cash capacity even after $26.6B of capital investment. Its current ratio of 0.8× is below 1.0, but that should not be read in isolation as distress: high sales frequency and short operating cycles allow large retailers to operate with structurally low current ratios. The follow-up question is whether capex is translating into durable productivity and digital-margin improvement rather than merely maintaining scale.

### 3. Target protects margin, but weaker sales and slower turns create the clearest execution risk

Target's latest net sales declined 2.0%, even though its operating margin of 4.9% was the highest of the three companies. Inventory turned only 6.0×, versus 9.3× at Walmart and 14.1× at Costco. Target still produced $2.8B of free cash flow and converted earnings into operating cash at 1.8×. [SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/27419/000002741926000016/tgt-20260131.htm)

**Implication.** Margin resilience is positive, but declining sales combined with lower inventory productivity can signal mix pressure, markdown exposure, weaker traffic, or excess capacity. The next investigation should separate comparable sales, traffic, average ticket, shrink, markdowns, advertising revenue, and category mix. Target's reported cash cycle is short, but it relies on annual receivable data; quarterly cash-cycle analysis remains unavailable rather than being estimated.

### 4. Reported profit is supported by cash across all three peers

OCF/net income was 1.7× for Costco, 1.8× for Target, and 1.9× for Walmart. Each ratio was above 1.0, and all three companies reported positive free cash flow. That reduces concern that current profit is primarily accrual-driven.

**Implication.** One year of strong conversion is supportive, not conclusive. Cash conversion can be temporarily lifted by inventory reductions, supplier timing, or tax and compensation accruals. The quarterly cash-flow bridge should therefore be monitored alongside the five-year trend before concluding that the latest conversion rate is sustainable.

### 5. Kroger shows what a grocery-led model looks like against the general-merchandise and club peers

Kroger's latest fiscal year net-sales growth of 0.4% ranked 3 of 4; operating margin of 1.3% ranked 4 of 4; inventory turnover of 16.3× ranked 1 of 4; cash conversion cycle (shorter is better) of -5.3 days ranked 1 of 4; OCF / net income of 7.2× ranked 1 of 4; ROIC of 8.3% ranked 4 of 4.

**Implication.** Grocery turns inventory faster and carries thinner margins than general merchandise, so Kroger should be read against Costco and Walmart's grocery mix rather than against Target. Kroger's cost of sales excludes depreciation and rent-type items, so its gross margin is not strictly comparable with peers that include them; operating margin and cash metrics are the cleaner cross-company comparison. Its net inventory is derived as FIFO inventory less the LIFO reserve because it does not tag a single net figure. OCF / net income is unusually high for KR (7.2×); a ratio that large usually reflects net income depressed by non-cash charges such as impairments, not exceptional cash generation, so it should not be read as a stronger cash-quality signal.

### 6. Returns differ because of capital intensity and leverage, not only margin

COST earned a 41.4% ROIC and 28.4% return on equity (3.0% net margin × 3.6× asset turnover × 2.6× equity multiplier); WMT earned a 16.8% ROIC and 23.0% return on equity (3.1% net margin × 2.6× asset turnover × 2.9× equity multiplier); TGT earned a 15.0% ROIC and 24.0% return on equity (3.5% net margin × 1.8× asset turnover × 3.8× equity multiplier); KR earned a 8.3% ROIC and 14.3% return on equity (0.7% net margin × 2.9× asset turnover × 7.2× equity multiplier).

**Implication.** ROIC isolates what the operating business earns on debt plus equity, while the DuPont split shows whether return on equity comes from margin, asset productivity, or balance-sheet leverage. A high ROE supported mainly by a larger equity multiplier deserves more scrutiny than one supported by margin or turnover. Invested capital here excludes operating leases, which understates the capital base of the store-heavy peers.

### 7. Capital allocation: payouts are funded differently across the peers

COST paid $16.4B in dividends and $3.6B in buybacks over 5 fiscal years (59% of free cash flow) and left $14.1B unallocated; KR paid $3.8B in dividends and $9.6B in buybacks over 5 fiscal years (102% of free cash flow) and overspent operating cash flow by $0.3B; TGT paid $9.5B in dividends and $11.2B in buybacks over 5 fiscal years (141% of free cash flow) and overspent operating cash flow by $6.0B; WMT paid $32.6B in dividends and $35.1B in buybacks over 5 fiscal years (103% of free cash flow) and overspent operating cash flow by $1.9B.

**Implication.** Payouts above free cash flow are sustainable only while balance-sheet capacity or cash on hand covers the gap, so the useful question is how much of the payout is discretionary buyback versus a committed dividend. Buybacks flex with conditions; dividends are sticky. A company that spends more than its operating cash flow on capex and payouts together is drawing on cash or debt, which also explains rising leverage in the DuPont decomposition. Costco's fiscal 2024 includes a one-off special dividend.

### 8. Leverage, coverage and leases

COST has net cash of $14.0B, interest coverage 113.4×, and lease adjustment lowers ROIC from 41.4% to 37.1%; KR has net debt of $12.5B (2.4× EBITDA), interest coverage 2.3×, and lease adjustment lowers ROIC from 8.3% to 7.2%; TGT has net debt of $11.0B (1.3× EBITDA), interest coverage 8.1×, and lease adjustment lowers ROIC from 15.0% to 13.6%; WMT has net debt of $34.0B (0.8× EBITDA), interest coverage 10.7×, and lease adjustment lowers ROIC from 16.8% to 15.6%.

**Implication.** Net debt to EBITDA and interest coverage describe capacity to keep returning capital; they matter most for the companies whose payouts already exceed free cash flow. Operating leases are a debt-like obligation that reported ROIC ignores, so lease-adjusted ROIC is the fairer comparison between store-heavy peers and a warehouse-club model with fewer leased sites. Coverage uses cash interest paid, which can be lumpy, and is unavailable on a trailing basis when quarterly filings omit it.

## Recommended areas for investigation

1. **Costco:** decompose growth into comparable sales, new warehouses, membership-fee growth, and foreign exchange; compare incremental operating profit with incremental capex.
2. **Walmart:** evaluate returns from store, supply-chain, automation, and e-commerce investment; normalize working-capital timing before extrapolating the latest cash conversion ratio.
3. **Target:** reconcile sales pressure with margin resilience through traffic, ticket, category mix, markdowns, shrink, and advertising/credit-card revenue; monitor inventory growth against sales each quarter.
4. **Kroger:** separate the effect of impairment and restructuring charges on operating margin, the buyback-driven fall in equity on ROE, and the contribution of pharmacy, fuel, and alternative profits to cash conversion.
5. **Across peers:** compare supplier-term changes and lease-adjusted obligations, then extend the analysis to segment-level and comparable-sales disclosures before making capital-allocation conclusions.

## Methodology, controls, and limitations

The latest validation run recorded **133 passed checks, 0 warnings, and 0 errors**. Revenue components reconcile for all displayed annual periods and quarters. Raw SEC responses, accession numbers, filing dates, source concepts, units, dimensions, and derived-fact status are retained.

- Net sales—not consolidated revenue—drives growth and gross margin. Membership and other revenue remains separate.
- Average beginning and ending balances drive inventory, receivable, and payable efficiency. Actual fiscal-day counts drive days metrics.
- Standalone quarterly flows are derived from cumulative disclosures when necessary; Q4 equals the annual value less Q3 YTD. Instant balances are never differenced.
- Fiscal years are aligned by calendar year of period end but are not identical economic windows. 3 displayed annual period(s) are 53-week years and are flagged rather than normalized away.
- Missing facts remain null; the current run has 0 warnings and no missing amount is silently treated as zero.
- Target has 12 missing quarterly cash-cycle observations because a comparable quarterly receivables series is not disclosed under the configured concepts.
- The analysis is consolidated-company, nominal USD, and U.S. GAAP. It excludes segments, constant-currency adjustments, leases beyond reported debt fields, valuation, and stock-price performance.
- Latest-known facts power the dashboard. Historical versions remain stored, but this report is not an as-of-date investment backtest.
